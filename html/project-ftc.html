01 / Systems

From floor to goal

Each mechanism handles one step: move into position, collect and sort, transfer, then aim and shoot.

Mecanum drivetrain

Four 312 RPM motors drive the mecanum wheels for strafing and rotation. We chose torque over top speed to help contest scoring areas, with PETG covers protecting the chassis and electronics.

Intake and color sorter

Belt-driven compliant wheels feed a horizontal rotating sorter, or “spindexer.” Four color sensors identify the game pieces so the robot can select the required shooting order.

Telescoping pusher

A three-stage, fully printed actuator lifts each selected game piece into the turret. Nested sections, springs, and a nylon line drive fit the transfer into the space beneath the sorter.

Vision-tracked turret

Two 6000 RPM motors power a single flywheel and hooded launcher. Two servos rotate the turret, while a mounted Limelight 3A camera tracks the goal independently of the chassis heading.

02 / Iteration

What testing changed

Match failures and prototype tests showed us where to simplify the mechanisms. Three changes made the biggest difference.

A shorter transfer path

The original transfer relied on the sorter to finish lifting each game piece, causing jams and breakages. Lowering the turret let the pusher feed it directly. Testing showed cycle time was cut in half.

More reliable collection

Early kickers struggled to grip game pieces. We switched to compliant wheels, revised the shaft spacing, and geared the intake down to 810 RPM. A fourth color sensor added redundancy when the intake sensor missed a piece.

Smoother goal tracking

An offset webcam made aiming inaccurate and tracking choppy. We centered a Limelight 3A on the turret for smoother tracking and reduced the hood angle to support shots closer to the goal.

03 / Build & control

Designed to be revised

We checked new parts in an Onshape master assembly before printing in PETG, then tested for interference and failure under load. Splitting the large printed body into four sections let us replace one area at a time, at the cost of some rigidity.

Sensing and autonomous control

Two odometry wheels and a goBILDA Pinpoint computer work with Road Runner for autonomous motion. Color sensing and AprilTag detection determine the shooting sequence; state machines coordinate intake and shooting. Our autonomous routines handle 6–9 sorted game pieces.

Flywheel velocity control and battery-voltage compensation address speed drops between shots and late in matches.

Tradeoffs and next steps

The 312 RPM drivetrain favors pushing power over speed. Faster motors remain a tradeoff to explore, alongside an adjustable launcher hood, a faster pusher servo, and more clearance around the intake to reduce stress on printed parts.
